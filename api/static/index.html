Overnight heat, measured street by street

Some neighbourhoods never cool down.

On one August night in Phoenix, 18 census tracts stayed above 28 °C from midnight until dawn. The air never once dropped below it. Four kilometres away, another tract spent most of the night below that line.

Heat kills at night, when the body needs a cool window to recover. The index cities use to decide where to help does not find these places.

Why the night is what matters

Two tracts, the same night, one line between them.

Afternoon maps show the whole city as uniformly hot, because at four o'clock it is. The difference appears after dark: some ground gives back the day's heat and some does not. Overnight minimum temperature predicts heat deaths better than the daytime peak, because a body that never cools never recovers.

What the number is

Hours of the night, midnight to dawn, that the outdoor air stayed above 28 °C (about 82 °F). Six hours means the whole window: it never once dropped below that line.

Why the night, not the day

The body sheds accumulated heat when the air is cooler than skin. A hot night removes that recovery window, so the strain from the previous day carries into the next. Overnight minimum temperature predicts heat deaths better than the daytime peak, which is why multi-day heat events are more lethal than single hot days.

Indoors is worse

A building that absorbed heat all day keeps releasing it inside for hours after the outside air drops. The readings here are outdoor air at head height, so the temperature people actually sleep in is generally higher.

The finding

Heat and disadvantage do not line up here.

Heat programmes are usually aimed using a social vulnerability index, which assumes the most vulnerable places are also the hottest. In this study area the two are unrelated: the correlation between overnight exposure and the CDC index is r = 0.004.

Each dot is one tract. If the assumption held, the dots would climb from left to right. They do not. That flat cloud is the finding.

What this does not show

The claims we tested and could not support.

These are here because a tool that only reports what it found is harder to trust than one that also says where it stops.

No intervention effects

We sampled surface composition at 50 tracts to test whether pavement and canopy explain overnight heat. Once position is accounted for, they do not: built surface gives t = +0.24, vegetation t = -1.46. So this tool never estimates how much cooling any intervention would buy.

Position, not mechanism

Latitude and longitude alone explain about 70% of the variation. Something about where a tract sits drives this, but we did not test what. Elevation, drainage and distance from open desert are all untested candidates.

A ceiling on the metric

The measure caps at six hours. Tracts at the cap were above 28 °C for the whole window and we cannot tell which was hottest, so they are not ranked against each other.
